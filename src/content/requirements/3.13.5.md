## Requirement

"Implement subnetworks for publicly accessible system components that are physically or logically separated from internal networks."

## Objectives

[a] publicly accessible system components are identified; and
[b] subnetworks for publicly accessible system components are physically or logically separated from internal networks.

## Sufficient

- **A firewall zone dedicated to any component reachable without credentials**, such as a remote-access gateway, with default-deny rules governing what that zone can reach on the internal network and only explicit exceptions allowed through. [a][b]
- **A cloud platform environment treated as inherently separated from on-premises infrastructure**, since the platform itself sits apart from the internal company network by design. [b]
- **An identity and access layer, such as a conditional-access gate, positioned in front of internal resources**, so that anything reachable before authentication is confined to the sign-in surface and nothing behind it is reachable without passing through that layer first. [a][b]
- **A dedicated subnet assigned to remote-access connections**, with its own address range and firewall rules governing what it can reach on the internal network, rather than remote users landing directly on the same subnet as internal systems. [a][b]
- **A documented, software-defined separation, such as a virtual network or access-controlled gateway, standing in for a traditional physical demilitarized zone.** Practitioner consensus and public cloud-security guidance both recognize this as a valid form of logical separation, not only a dedicated physical network segment. [b]

## Insufficient

- **Access control lists offered as the sole basis for separation, with no accompanying network-level boundary.** A public-facing component's traffic still needs somewhere to be filtered at the network layer; relying only on which accounts can log in does not, by itself, satisfy every reading of what this requirement expects. [b]
- **Multi-factor authentication or device authentication offered as the entire answer, with no underlying network separation.** Authentication controls who gets in; this requirement is about where the component sits relative to the internal network, which authentication alone does not address. [b]

## Over-engineering

- **Building an elaborate, custom segmented infrastructure for a purely cloud-hosted environment with no on-premises servers at all.** The platform's own separation from the rest of the company's infrastructure already does the job; adding a parallel homegrown demilitarized zone on top of it is unnecessary. [b]
- **Bringing a third-party-hosted static website, run entirely outside the organization's own network, into the documented boundary as if it needed a matching internal separation.** Noting it as out of scope, with the third-party hosting itself providing the separation, is enough. [a][b]

## Edge cases

- **What actually counts as "publicly accessible."** A login page or connection entry point reachable without credentials is publicly accessible even though everything behind it requires authentication; a common early misreading treats only fully unauthenticated resources as public, when in fact the reachable front door of an otherwise protected system, such as a remote-access login page, already counts and needs to sit in its own separated zone.
- **Whether access control lists alone can satisfy this requirement in place of a dedicated network zone.** One position treats the requirement's reference to "subnetworks" as an older way of describing separation, and accepts a well-designed access control list as meeting its intent on a modern network; the opposing position treats this requirement as the demilitarized-zone requirement specifically and does not accept access control alone as a substitute for network-level separation. The outcome depends on the assessor.
- **Whether a phone system reachable by outside callers counts as a publicly accessible system component.** This question has been raised without a settled answer; organizations with such a system should be prepared to document their reasoning either way.
- **Whether a cloud identity platform's sign-in surface needs its own documented separation entry, even when the underlying platform already keeps it apart from internal resources architecturally.** One position treats the platform's built-in separation as sufficient on its own; a more conservative position still expects the organization to describe, in its own documentation, what that sign-in surface is and how it is separated, rather than relying entirely on an inherited platform characteristic.
- **A mail server with a public-facing web interface disabled and all mail client access routed through a remote-access connection.** Whether this configuration alone satisfies the separation requirement, or needs additional documented network-level controls, has been raised without a definitive answer.

## Evidence examples

- **A network diagram showing separate zones or subnets for public-facing components.** [a][b]
- **Firewall configuration showing a remote-access or public-facing zone with default-deny rules toward the internal network.** [b]
- **Cloud architecture documentation showing an identity and access gate positioned ahead of internal resources.** [a][b]
- **A list of identified publicly accessible components**, such as a remote-access gateway or an internet-facing web server. [a]

## Related requirements

- **3.13.1** covers boundary protection generally; this requirement is the specific rule that publicly reachable components must sit on their own separated subnetwork within that broader boundary picture.
- **3.13.6** supplies the deny-by-default, allow-by-exception rule that a separated public-access zone typically relies on to control what it can reach internally.
- **3.1.20** governs external connections, which frequently intersects with which components end up classified as publicly accessible here.
