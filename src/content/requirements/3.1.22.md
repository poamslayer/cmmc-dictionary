## Requirement

"Control information posted or processed on publicly accessible information systems."

## Objectives

- [a] individuals authorized to post or process information on publicly accessible systems are identified;
- [b] procedures to ensure FCI is not posted or processed on publicly accessible systems are identified;
- [c] a review process is in place prior to posting of any content to publicly accessible systems;
- [d] content on publicly accessible systems is reviewed to ensure that it does not include FCI; and
- [e] mechanisms are in place to remove and address improper posting of FCI.

## Sufficient

- **Name in policy who may post to an organization-controlled publicly accessible system**, such as a marketing or communications role, and require a documented review before anything goes live from that role. [a][c]
- **Review already-published content on a defined cadence, at minimum once a year**, and produce a signed or dated record naming who reviewed it and when; this cadence has carried at least one organization through a federal assessment. [d]
- **Reserve this requirement for systems the organization actually controls the posting on**, such as its own website and its own official social media pages, and exclude an employee's personal social media account from the pre-posting review process, addressing personal posting instead through security awareness training. [a][b]
- **Use a change-detection tool that flags when a hosted page changes** so review effort concentrates on what actually changed rather than the entire site on a fixed schedule, paired with the required pre-publication step so both the before and after review objectives are covered. [c][d]
- **For an environment where a very large population could technically post to a shared public domain**, restrict the practical review scope to the organization's own controlled corner of that domain, relying on separate access controls to keep the wider population away from anything containing controlled information in the first place. [c][d]

## Insufficient

- **Having a written social media or posting policy with no evidence that a periodic review of already-published content actually happens**: a federal assessment specifically pushed past the policy documents to ask for proof of an ongoing review. [d]
- **Having the same individual both post content and perform the review of that content with no separation between the two roles**: this drew explicit pushback from an assessor perspective, even though a very small organization may be treated more leniently on this specific point. [c][d]
- **Reviewing only after content is already live, with no documented step confirming review happened before publication**: this requirement has a distinct before-posting objective that an after-the-fact check alone does not satisfy. [c]

## Over-engineering

- **Producing a separate documented review record for every individual post** rather than treating review as a standard, consistently followed step within the existing publishing process. [c]
- **Extending the pre-posting review requirement to employees' personal social media accounts**: that is addressed through security awareness training and rules of behavior, not through this requirement's review process. [a]
- **Reviewing an entire large shared public domain when only a small, organization-controlled portion of it is actually relevant**, rather than scoping the review to that controlled portion and relying on separate access controls for the rest. [d]

## Edge cases

- The Assessment Guide's detailed objective language for this requirement is written in terms of FCI, reflecting its origin as a Level 1 practice, while the same requirement carries into Level 2 where CUI is the greater concern; the practical answer taken in the field is to write policy and reviews that cover both terms rather than picking one.
- Whether one person may both post and review without a documented separation of duties is treated differently by different assessors; one view holds this is fine for a very small organization with no stated requirement for separation, while another view holds that no assessor would be comfortable with it, and the outcome depends on the assessor.
- Whether an unintended disclosure through a channel the organization deliberately scoped out of its CUI environment, such as an email system kept outside that environment, still needs its own detection-and-response process is answered yes: the same logic that requires a process to prevent and respond to improper disclosure applies to any channel capable of an accidental one, even a scoped-out one.

## Evidence examples

- Written policy identifying who may post to the organization's public website or social media accounts. [a]
- Documented pre-publication review workflow, whether a ticketing system, a shared tracking sheet, or a version-control review step. [c]
- Dated, signed annual, or more frequent, attestation that a reviewer checked the live site for improperly posted controlled information. [d]
- Incident or spillage response procedure describing how improperly posted information gets removed and who is notified. [e]
- Training record showing personnel who post content were trained to recognize controlled information before it goes live. [a][b]

## Related requirements

- 3.2.1 and the organization's rules-of-behavior training cover the personal social media use this requirement's review process does not reach.
- 3.8.1 and 3.8.2 protect CUI media more generally, of which an accidental public posting is one failure mode this requirement's response mechanism addresses.
- 3.1.20 covers the broader question of external systems, distinct from this requirement's narrower focus on systems the organization itself controls for public posting.
