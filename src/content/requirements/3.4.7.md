## Requirement

"Restrict, disable, or prevent the use of nonessential programs, functions, ports, protocols, and services."

## Objectives

[a] essential programs are defined;
[b] the use of nonessential programs is defined;
[c] the use of nonessential programs is restricted, disabled, or prevented as defined;
[d] essential functions are defined;
[e] the use of nonessential functions is defined;
[f] the use of nonessential functions is restricted, disabled, or prevented as defined;
[g] essential ports are defined;
[h] the use of nonessential ports is defined;
[i] the use of nonessential ports is restricted, disabled, or prevented as defined;
[j] essential protocols are defined;
[k] the use of nonessential protocols is defined;
[l] the use of nonessential protocols is restricted, disabled, or prevented as defined;
[m] essential services are defined;
[n] the use of nonessential services is defined; and
[o] the use of nonessential services is restricted, disabled, or prevented as defined.

## Sufficient

- **Treating ports, protocols, and services as one combined management topic rather than three separate lists**, with a three-tier classification: essential items needed to meet a requirement (a specific transport-layer encryption version, for example), restricted items allowed only through an approved and documented network traffic rule in a deny-by-default architecture, and prohibited items blocked outright as a standalone rule so they cannot be accidentally allowed back in through an approved rule. This grouped structure has been treated as an acceptable way to satisfy the ports/protocols/services objectives together. [g][h][i][j][k][l][m][n][o]
- **Blocking a well-known insecure port at the firewall**, which in practice blocks the associated protocol and service at the same time and is accepted as covering all three together for that item. [i][l][o]
- **A default-deny firewall posture: blocking incoming traffic on all ports and blocking outgoing traffic except on an explicitly approved list.** [g][h][i]
- **A blacklist-style definition where everything is treated as essential except what is explicitly blocked**, rather than requiring every single item to be individually justified as essential before use is allowed. This deny-by-exception framing has been accepted as compliant under the current version of the requirement. [g][h][i][j][k][l][m][n][o]
- **Functions defined at a task level (reading, editing, and saving documents; sending and receiving work-related email) as essential, with named categories such as online gambling, gaming, or a music player named as nonessential**, and technically restrictable items (Bluetooth, printing) called out specifically where they can be turned off. [d][e][f]
- **A program-by-program review asking a simple question of each entry on an installed-programs list: is this needed for work?** Anything answered no gets turned off; games are a commonly cited example of a program removed this way. [a][b][c]
- **Documentation kept either inside the system security plan or in a separate referenced policy or procedure document**, with either location accepted as long as it is available and provided to the assessor. [a][d][g][j][m]

## Insufficient

No documented position beyond the Assessment Guide; see confidence.

## Over-engineering

- **Attempting to individually classify every operating-system service as essential or nonessential.** A default installation can easily run on the order of a hundred background services, many of which are not well understood even by experienced staff; treating this item-by-item exercise as mandatory for every single service has been called out as impractical and is not what the objective requires when a grouped or blacklist-style approach is used instead. [m][n][o]
- **Insisting on separate bins for ports, protocols, and services rather than managing them as one combined list.** Distinguishing a protocol from a service in the abstract is a common source of confusion with little practical payoff; a combined ports/protocols/services management approach satisfies the same objectives.

## Edge cases

- **Whether a port merely being open counts as "use," separate from whether traffic is actually observed on it.** One position treats an open port as distinct from active use of that port, which would let an organization argue a rarely used but open port is not "in use" in the sense this requirement means; the more common approach defines "use of nonessential" as simply the inverse of "essential," collapsing that distinction even though it can feel redundant to state it that way. Which reading controls is not settled, and the outcome depends on the assessor.
- **What to do with services nobody on staff fully understands, where disabling one risks breaking the system.** The practical answer favored in practice is the blacklist framing: define everything as essential except what is explicitly prohibited, rather than requiring a justification for every unfamiliar service before it can remain enabled.
- **Device-level review versus network-level review of services.** Both are valid and are treated as two different zoom levels on the same objective: device-level review (checking what is running locally on an endpoint) becomes part of the baseline configuration, while network-level review (what is reachable across the network) is controlled through firewall rules; an organization is expected to cover both, not pick one.

- **Firewall rule set** showing default-deny inbound and an approved-exception outbound list. [g][h][i][j][k][l]
- **List of essential programs required for work**, reviewed against what is actually installed. [a][b][c]
- **List of essential functions** (document handling, project email) with named nonessential functions to restrict. [d][e][f]
- **Policy naming prohibited services** such as gaming or gambling applications. [m][n][o]
- **A standard device-provisioning script** that removes unneeded defaults before a system reaches a user, rather than shipping a vanilla installation. [a][b][c][m][n][o]
- **Group policy or registry configuration** controlling which local services run on a device. [m][n][o]

## Related requirements

- **3.4.6** is the broader principle this requirement narrows down: where 3.4.6 defines essential capabilities in general, this requirement specifically requires restricting the programs, functions, ports, protocols, and services that fall outside that definition.
- **3.4.1** and **3.4.2** supply the inventory and baseline that this requirement's essential/nonessential definitions get written into as configuration items.
- **3.13.1** and the boundary-protection requirements often share the same firewall rule evidence used here for ports and protocols.
