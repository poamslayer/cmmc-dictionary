## Requirement

"Control the flow of CUI in accordance with approved authorizations."

## Objectives

[a] information flow control policies are defined;
[b] methods and enforcement mechanisms for controlling the flow of CUI are defined;
[c] designated sources and destinations (e.g., networks, individuals, and devices) for CUI within the system and between interconnected systems are identified;
[d] authorizations for controlling the flow of CUI are defined; and
[e] approved authorizations for controlling the flow of CUI are enforced.

## Sufficient

- **A written policy describing how CUI may be shared, backed by recurring training on that policy**, accepted for small organizations as the primary control, without a matching technical enforcement layer. [a][b]
- **A data flow diagram identifying, briefly, the source and destination of every place CUI moves inside the organization.** Accepted as strong evidence for the sources-and-destinations objective. [c]
- **Email data loss prevention that prompts for a justification whenever a CUI-labeled message goes out, notifies IT of the send, and logs sender, recipient, and justification.** Accepted, with the caveat that the people who authorize, implement, and monitor that workflow should not all be the same person. [b][d][e]
- **Sensitivity labeling and policy enforcement through an information-protection platform**, blocking download to unmanaged drives, blocking sharing with unapproved recipients, and forcing encryption in transit. [b][e]
- **Combining written policy with technical controls**, such as disabling removable storage, disabling copy-paste on unmanaged or personal devices, and requiring encryption, alongside contractual flow-down of the same expectations to subcontractors. [a][b][e]
- **A list of approved sources and destinations that covers only where CUI actually goes**, not a record of every network destination users might reach; ordinary outbound web traffic on standard ports does not need to be enumerated. [c]

## Insufficient

- **Treating the Assessment Guide's own worked example as a complete answer.** The Guide's example only covers CUI moving from the organization to a prime contractor; it says nothing about internal flows or flows to a subcontractor, and the Guide itself states its examples are illustrations, not a checklist to copy. [a][c][d]
- **Policy alone, with no technical control, in a large organization with many users, devices, and volumes of CUI.** A policy-only answer is more likely to be accepted at small scale; at larger scale, some technical enforcement is expected alongside it, and the outcome depends on the assessor. [b][e]

## Over-engineering

- **Blocking all public internet access.** This is not required; the requirement is about where CUI goes, not about locking down every outbound connection. One attempt to build an allowlist covering only a government cloud and a handful of approved sites was described as a large, unnecessary undertaking.
- **Building an allowlist of every external sender permitted to send inbound email.** The requirement is about designated CUI sources and destinations, not a roster of every possible sender; this was rejected as out of scope for the objective.
- **Blocking access to personal webmail sites.** There is no requirement compelling this; a policy prohibiting sending CUI through unapproved channels accomplishes the same goal without the technical lockdown.

## Edge cases

- **Virtual desktop or contractor risk managed asset architectures** that keep CUI off the broader commercial environment are a legitimate approach for larger organizations, but the cost of building and maintaining that separation should be weighed against how much of the rest of the requirement set still has to be implemented on the systems that remain outside the virtual environment. [c]
- **Export-controlled or specified CUI categories** are sometimes handled by applying the strictest applicable flow controls to the whole CUI population, rather than differentiating handling by category, when the categories cannot be cleanly separated technically.
- **Access control list enforcement at the network perimeter versus at internal segment boundaries.** Only the boundary of the assessment scope strictly requires logical separation; internal boundaries can rely on lighter controls such as share permissions where the residual risk is lower. [e]
- **Whether a policy-only answer is sufficient for blocking uploads of CUI to internet sites depends on organizational size and the volume of CUI involved.** Smaller organizations have had a policy-and-training answer accepted, while an assessor evaluating a larger environment may expect a technical control, and the outcome depends on the assessor.
- **Separation of duties inside a data-loss-prevention workflow.** When a single tool authorizes, implements, and can also review its own logs, the people behind those three functions should not be the same individual, echoing the separation-of-duties requirement.

## Evidence examples

- **Information flow control policy document** covering objectives [a], [b], and [d].
- **A diagram tracing where CUI enters, moves within, and exits the environment.** [c]
- **Access control list for a CUI document library or file share.** [d][e]
- **Data loss prevention configuration** restricting where CUI-labeled email can go. [e]
- **Training records showing staff were trained on the flow-control policy.** [b]
- **Policy language including a disciplinary consequence for violations**, supporting enforcement. [e]

## Related requirements

- **3.1.4** (separation of duties) is directly implicated whenever a single technical workflow authorizes, implements, and monitors CUI flow at once.
- **3.1.20** covers external connections generally; this requirement is the CUI-specific subset of that broader question.
- **3.13.16** (protecting CUI at rest) and the encryption-in-transit expectation here work together wherever CUI is moving between systems.
