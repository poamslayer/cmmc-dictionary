## Requirement

"Identify, report, and correct information and information system flaws in a timely manner."

## Objectives

[a] the time within which to identify system flaws is specified;
[b] system flaws are identified within the specified time frame;
[c] the time within which to report system flaws is specified;
[d] system flaws are reported within the specified time frame;
[e] the time within which to correct system flaws is specified; and
[f] system flaws are corrected within the specified time frame.

## Sufficient

- **A point-in-time vulnerability or configuration scan report**, treated as the most common baseline evidence that flaws were identified. [a][b]
- **A ticket, email, or similar record directed at whoever performs remediation**, naming a flaw and requesting a fix, accepted as reporting evidence as long as the record can be traced back to a specific flaw. [c][d]
- **A tracking log or spreadsheet with unremediated and remediated states**, moving each item across as it closes with dates attached, giving a defensible before-and-after trail. [b][d][f]
- **Patch management tool deployment history**, showing what was installed and when, standing in as correction evidence. [f]
- **A scanning tool configured to run on a fixed interval**, such as every seven days, satisfying the defined-frequency objectives for identification. [a][b]
- **A written, severity-based service level for correcting flaws**, giving objective [e] a defined timeframe rather than an undefined expectation. [e]
- **A rolling or major-version designation in the software inventory for continuously updating applications**, such as a browser, reflecting how the software actually deploys rather than forcing a fixed version number that goes stale immediately. [f]

## Insufficient

- **No distinct record that an identified flaw was communicated to whoever acts on it**, even when the same person both scans and patches. Skipping a documented reporting step fails objectives [c] and [d] regardless of how well the identification and correction steps are otherwise handled. [c][d]
- **Treating the requirement as only its correction objective**, while never specifying or demonstrating the separate timeframes for identifying and reporting flaws. Assessment Guide discussion lists identification, reporting, and correction as three distinct timeframes that each need their own definition and evidence. [a][c]
- **Evidence with no traceable link back to a specific flaw or scan result**, such as a general patch log that does not reference what it was addressing, leaving the connection between identification and correction unproven. [b][d]

## Over-engineering

- **Routing every patch, including routine operating system updates, through a formal change advisory board before installation.** This adds delay without adding assurance; a documented standard-change policy for low-risk, recurring updates is accepted without a per-patch approval cycle. [f]
- **A multi-step formal review workflow specifically built for operating system patches**, when a simpler documented schedule, such as a defined test-ring and then production-ring rollout, already satisfies the defined-timeframe objectives on its own. [e][f]

## Edge cases

- **A single-person IT function struggles to show a distinct reporting step**, since the same individual identifies, decides on, and applies the fix. Practitioner consensus resolves this by inserting a documented reporting artifact, such as a dated list of needed patches created before remediation begins, so objectives [c] and [d] have evidence independent of a second reviewer. [c][d]
- **Flaws are not limited to vulnerabilities.** A configuration weakness surfaced by a hardening benchmark counts as a flaw requiring the same identify-report-correct handling as a missing patch. [a][f]
- **A system that cannot be patched without breaking a needed function, with no alternate system available**, is commonly handled through a documented enduring exception or a multi-year plan of action rather than left as an indefinitely unremediated flaw.
- **Where a customer's own approved-software list blocks installing an available update**, whether an open-ended correction date is acceptable evidence has no settled answer in the record reviewed for this file; the outcome depends on the assessor.
- This requirement is commonly failed at assessment when organizations read it too narrowly, addressing only correction and leaving identification and reporting timeframes undocumented.

## Evidence examples

- **Dated vulnerability or configuration scan reports** produced at the defined interval. [a][b]
- **Ticket or email records** connecting an identified flaw to the person responsible for remediation. [c][d]
- **A tracking spreadsheet or log** with unremediated and remediated columns, timestamped as items move between them. [b][d][f]
- **Patch management tool deployment history** showing installed updates and dates. [f]
- **Written policy stating identification, reporting, and correction timeframes**, including any severity-based tiering. [a][c][e]
- **Software inventory entries using a rolling or major-version designation** for continuously updating applications, reconciled against the 3.4.1 baseline. [f]

## Related requirements

- **3.11.2** supplies the vulnerability scan results that often serve as this requirement's identification step.
- **3.11.3** covers the risk-based prioritization that decides how urgently an identified flaw needs remediation.
- **3.4.1** requires a baseline software inventory that a rolling-version approach to patching must stay reconciled with.
- **3.14.5** covers malicious code scanning; a flaw it surfaces flows into this requirement's identify-report-correct cycle.
