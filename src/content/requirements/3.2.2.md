## Requirement

"Ensure that personnel are trained to carry out their assigned information security-related duties and responsibilities."

## Objectives

[a] information security-related duties, roles, and responsibilities are defined;
[b] information security-related duties, roles, and responsibilities are assigned to designated personnel; and
[c] personnel are adequately trained to carry out their assigned information security-related duties, roles, and responsibilities.

## Sufficient

- **The organization sets its own bar for what training satisfies a role, rather than any external body mandating a credential.** A certification is one option among several; conference attendance, coursework, an online module, a vendor training video, or a government-sourced course can all count as the defined training for a role. [a][c]
- **A simple role-to-duty mapping written in plain language**, for example naming who handles access control across the domain, firewall, and VPN, who handles configuration of those same systems, and who reviews the logs, has been confirmed as an acceptable way to satisfy the definition-and-assignment pair. [a][b]
- **Pointing designated staff to specific policies, procedures, and training material by system component**, with a signature confirming they received and reviewed the material for their assigned piece. [b][c]
- **A tracking spreadsheet or matrix** showing each role, the training tied to it, who holds the role, and when training was completed. [b][c]
- **Existing professional certifications used as the defined training requirement for a role**, when the organization designates them as such; certifications carrying a continuing-education requirement help demonstrate the training stays current. [a][c]
- **Reading the three objectives as a sequence**: name the duty, assign it to someone, and confirm the training for it, gives a workable structure for organizing the evidence. [a][b][c]

## Insufficient

- **General organization-wide awareness training treated as though it also covers this requirement.** Awareness training answers 3.2.1; this requirement expects at least some infosec-specific duties defined and evidence of training tied to those specific duties, even in a very small organization.
- **A government-sourced mandatory module used as the entire answer.** It does not tell staff who to report an incident to inside the organization, how to handle CUI in that organization's own environment, or what their specific role responsibilities are, so it does not reach [a] through [c] on its own.
- **A third-party training subscription treated as a complete program with nothing organization-specific layered on top.** The role-specific duties, responsibilities, and procedures particular to the organization still need their own training tied to them.
- **Pointing to hiring standards as training evidence**, for example arguing that competent people were hired so no further training is needed. A pile of resumes from candidates who were not hired is not evidence that the people who were hired were trained for their assigned duties.

## Over-engineering

- **Requiring a completion certificate for every session as a hard pass or fail line.** Formal proof-of-completion is common government audit practice, but it is not itself named in the assessment objectives; treating its absence alone as a finding reaches further than the objectives ask for. A simpler completion note, such as a confirmation the material was reviewed, can be enough.
- **Mandating formal, continuing-education-bearing certifications for every role in a small organization.** Simpler options, such as a government-sourced course or internally written material with a signed review, satisfy the same objectives at far lower cost.

## Edge cases

- **Facility staff such as janitorial personnel working under a third-party contract.** One position holds that cleaning and restocking duties carry no information-security responsibility at all, aside from general facility-security habits like not holding doors for strangers. A second position holds that keeping people out at entry points is itself a security-relevant duty. A third position holds that any unescorted person is a potential CUI touchpoint, since they may encounter CUI in a printer tray, on a screen, or in the trash, and should be trained accordingly; the resolution in practice tracks how the environment is scoped, so where other controls already keep CUI away from unescorted staff, this population may fall outside the training obligation. The outcome depends on the assessor.
- **Whether this requirement reaches every person with CUI access or only people carrying a defined information-security role.** The organization gets to draw that line itself; one accepted position is that everyone carries at least a baseline duty, such as reporting a concern to security, while dedicated infosec roles get the deeper role-based training this requirement is centered on.
- **Certifications that are old relative to a fast-moving discipline.** A credential earned many years ago does not by itself demonstrate current knowledge of how to secure a modern environment; continuing-education requirements attached to a certification help close that gap.

## Evidence examples

- **A tracking matrix** listing roles, required training, completion dates, and the people assigned. [a][b][c]
- **Stored certification records** for personnel whose role training is defined as a credential. [a][c]
- **Signed confirmations** that designated staff received and reviewed the policy, procedure, or training material tied to their role. [b][c]
- **A list of training material organized by system component**, naming the document, video, or course tied to each. [a][c]
- **Records pulled from a learning-management or HR system** showing completion. [c]
- **Completion records from a government-sourced role-relevant course.** [c]

## Related requirements

- **3.2.1** is the organization-wide awareness layer everyone receives; this requirement is the narrower, role-specific training layer for people carrying information-security duties.
- **3.1.5** and **3.1.7** define the privileged and least-privilege access this requirement's trained roles typically hold; the training exists to make sure the people with that access know how to use it correctly.
- **3.12.4**, the SSP, is where the organization's defined roles and responsibilities are typically recorded, giving this requirement's objective [a] a documented home.
