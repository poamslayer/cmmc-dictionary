## Requirement

"Separate the duties of individuals to reduce the risk of malevolent activity without collusion."

## Objectives

[a] the duties of individuals requiring separation are defined;
[b] responsibilities for duties that require separation are assigned to separate individuals; and
[c] access privileges that enable individuals to exercise the duties that require separation are granted to separate individuals.

## Sufficient

- **A three-person model for very small IT teams**, where one person holds administrative rights and handles day-to-day configuration but needs a second person's approval for changes and does not review logs; a second person approves configuration changes, holds no access to CUI, and does not review logs; and a third person also holds administrative rights, has no CUI access, and reviews logs for everyone including the first person. This pattern has been confirmed as sufficient by an assessor. [a][b][c]
- **A minimum two-person arrangement**, where each person reviews the other's activity and logs, so that no single administrator's actions go unchecked, meets the intent for the smallest organizations. [b][c]
- **Bringing in an outside party for a limited number of hours a month** to perform the auditing function, even part time, creates a legitimate separation between the people doing the work and the person checking it. [b][c]
- **A family-run or closely held business dividing tasks between family members**, provided the division is documented and each person can demonstrate real competence and authority for their assigned duty, not just a title. [a][b]
- **Approval workflows requiring a documented justification before an elevated role activates**, reviewed after the fact by someone other than the requester, paired with alerting that would flag tampering with the underlying logs. [b][c]
- **Relying on the immutability of cloud identity platform logs** (they cannot be deleted, only expired under a retention policy) as part of the argument that log review by a second person is meaningful. [c]
- **Documenting roles by what each person is restricted from doing, not only by what they are assigned to do.** [a]

## Insufficient

- **Assigning duties by convenience or family relationship rather than demonstrated ability to perform them.** An assessor wants to see that the person doing the separated duty actually has the competence and the authority to do it, not just a role on paper. [a][b]
- **Defining separation of duties on paper that is never actually exercised.** If the person supposedly separated from a decision has never been seen using that authority, for example formally rejecting a change, the separation looks unreal. [b][c]
- **One IT person performing everything with no external check at all.** A single administrator with no outside review, however small the company, fails the intent of the requirement. [b][c]
- **A vague, general statement about oversight instead of naming specific duties that require separation.** The requirement gives the organization the power to define which duties need separating, and a broad, non-specific statement does not use that power. [a]
- **Reviewing your own log output with no second reviewer.** Self-review removes the check the requirement is built around. [c]

## Over-engineering

No documented position beyond the Assessment Guide; see confidence.

## Edge cases

- **Two-person married or family-run businesses.** Longevity of the relationship is not, by itself, a substitute for a real separation; the organization must still show formal separation of authorizing, doing, and auditing, even at the smallest scale. [a][b][c]
- **Cross-training so that each of two people audits the other's domain.** This only works if each person genuinely lacks access to the domain they are auditing; if either can reach into the other's area, the separation is not real. [c]
- **Splitting a duty functionally rather than by person** (for example, separating who writes a policy from who enforces it) is an accepted alternative pattern to splitting purely by named role. [a]
- **Making an audit step a joint activity that both people must attend.** This is a workable but harder-to-verify alternative to assigning the audit to one clearly separate person. [b]
- **The circular risk of who audits the auditor.** Whichever role performs the audit function could, in principle, use administrative access to cover its own actions; an outside party or a genuinely walled-off second reviewer is the accepted way out of that loop. [c]
- **Two administrators who each hold full administrative rights** can satisfy the requirement for a small company if they function as checks on each other, one focused on implementation and one on verification, with a third person holding final approval authority. [b][c]
- **A non-technical oversight role.** A reviewer who lacks deep technical skill can still perform the separated duty by approving or rejecting access requests and reviewing the output of a log review, without personally understanding every technical detail. [b]

## Evidence examples

- **Change management records showing who approved a change versus who implemented it.** [b][c]
- **Log review records naming a reviewer different from the person being reviewed.** [c]
- **Training records demonstrating the competence of the person assigned a separated duty.** [a]
- **Access approval records with a documented justification**, from a privileged access management workflow. [b][c]
- **Written role definitions listing responsibilities and restrictions, not only permissions.** [a]
- **A documented instance of an approver rejecting a change**, showing the authority is real. [b]
- **Recurring log reviews with evidence of supervisor or director sign-off.** [c]

## Related requirements

- **3.1.5** (least privilege) and **3.1.7** (privileged functions) work alongside this requirement: separation of duties decides who holds which access, and those requirements decide how narrowly that access is scoped.
- **3.3.1** and **3.3.2** (audit logging) supply the records that make the auditing half of this requirement possible to verify.
- **3.12.1** ties into the assessment side: whoever reviews control effectiveness should not be the same person implementing the controls being reviewed.
