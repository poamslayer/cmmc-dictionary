## Requirement

"Employ the principle of least privilege, including for specific security functions and privileged accounts."

## Objectives

[a] privileged accounts are identified;
[b] access to privileged accounts is authorized in accordance with the principle of least privilege;
[c] security functions are identified; and
[d] access to security functions is authorized in accordance with the principle of least privilege.

## Sufficient

- **Two accounts per person, one privileged (domain administrator level) and one standard**, accepted for a very small organization (around three people), with the domain administrator account defined as the organization's privileged account type. [a][b]
- **A privileged-access-management workflow in a cloud identity platform** (eligible-role assignment with time-limited activation) is an accepted way to keep privileged access authorized on a least-privilege basis rather than standing. [b][d]
- **A separate naming convention distinguishing standard accounts from administrative accounts** for the same person, demonstrating that the two access levels are actually kept apart, not just conceptually separate. [a][b]
- **Defining the privileged-account category narrowly**, as long as some account type is actually identified as privileged; claiming no privileged accounts exist at all does not hold up, because some accounts always exist to run the organization's IT. [a]

## Insufficient

- **Using a domain administrator account for tasks that do not require that level of access.** This inverts the principle into something closer to most privilege, and an assessor is likely to flag routine tasks performed with an account that has organization-wide reach. [b][d]
- **Asserting that no privileged accounts exist in the environment.** Every organization has some account type that runs and configures its systems; the category can be defined narrowly, but it cannot be defined out of existence. [a]
- **Installing software on an individual workstation using a domain-wide administrative account.** If that software carries malware, the blast radius extends to everything the domain account can reach, rather than staying contained to the one machine. [b]

## Over-engineering

- **Maintaining three separate account tiers (domain administrator, local administrator, and standard user) for a very small organization.** Two tiers, privileged and standard, are enough at that scale; a third tier adds administrative overhead without a matching gain against this specific requirement, even though tiered administration remains a good general security practice independent of what the requirement itself demands.

## Edge cases

- **Very small organizations (roughly three people).** Two account types per person, privileged and standard, is the accepted floor; the privileged type can be defined as broadly as the organization's own domain administrator role, as long as it is documented as such. [a][b]
- **IT functions handled entirely by an outside provider.** The organization does not necessarily use privileged accounts directly, but it still must identify what privileged access exists in its environment, even when a third party operates it, and document the security functions that provider enforces. [a][c]
- **Access to government-hosted identity or personnel systems through certificate-based authentication.** Holding a credential into an external government system does not, by itself, make that credential a privileged account under this requirement; the relevant question is whether the account enforces security policy inside the organization's own environment. An administrative role inside the organization's own tenant is a privileged account regardless. [a]
- **Service or non-human accounts.** Whether this requirement's privileged-account logic extends to service accounts the same way it does to human accounts was left an open question in practitioner discussion, with no settled position; treat it as a gap to resolve with an assessor rather than an established rule.

## Evidence examples

- **Privileged access management configuration** showing dedicated administrative roles with time-limited or approval-gated activation. [b][d]
- **Account listing showing the naming pattern separating standard from administrative accounts for the same person.** [a][b]
- **Written definition of what the organization treats as a privileged account in its own environment.** [a]
- **A list of identified privileged accounts with the justification for each.** [a][b]

## Related requirements

- **3.1.6** and **3.1.7** depend directly on this requirement's definitions: 3.1.6 covers what happens when someone with a privileged account performs a non-security task, and 3.1.7 covers preventing non-privileged users from reaching privileged functions at all.
- **3.1.4** (separation of duties) and this requirement are often implemented through the same privileged-access-management tooling, since both rely on defining who holds elevated access and requiring approval to use it.
