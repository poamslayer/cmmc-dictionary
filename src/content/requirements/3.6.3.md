## Requirement

"Test the organizational incident response capability."

## Objectives

[a] the incident response capability is tested.

## Sufficient

- **Checklist walkthroughs, tabletop exercises, and parallel or full-interrupt simulations are all recognized testing formats.** A comprehensive live exercise is one option among several, not the only path to a MET finding. [a]
- **A realistic scenario that walks the team through discovery, documentation, escalation, and containment, checked against the organization's own written procedures, including filling out the forms that would actually be used.** This exercises the capability rather than only discussing it in the abstract. [a]
- **A test that also exercises the ability to reach outside help, to notify external parties, and to confirm that logging would actually surface the scenario's activity, closing with documented lessons learned and any resulting change requests.** This is the fuller version of what a sufficient test covers, beyond the technical walkthrough alone. [a]
- **No dedicated incident-response specialists are required to run the test.** The staff who would actually respond to a live incident, typically the regular system administration team, can conduct the exercise themselves. [a]
- **A practical floor of at least one test per year, with quarterly testing treated as a stronger practice.** The requirement itself sets no explicit minimum frequency, but this is the commonly applied assessor expectation. [a]

## Insufficient

- **A written incident response plan and supporting policies that have never actually been exercised through any kind of drill.** This requirement's objective asks whether the capability has been tested, not whether it has been documented; policies that exist only on paper are a commonly failed pattern at assessment. [a]
- **A test that is run once and produces no record of what was learned, with no follow-up change requests when gaps surface.** A session that corrects nothing going forward reads as unexercised in substance even though it occurred. [a]

## Over-engineering

- **Specialized incident-response contractors, elaborate red-team engagements, or purpose-built exercise software.** A scenario-driven discussion led by the people who would actually respond, using the organization's own procedures and forms, meets the objective at far lower cost than a formal, vendor-run exercise. [a]

## Edge cases

- **Whether responding to an actual incident can itself count as the test for this objective is unresolved.** One position holds that no assessment objective specifically requires a tabletop exercise or a documented after-action review, so a well-handled real incident should count; the counter position reads the Assessment Guide's list of specific testing formats as expecting a deliberate exercise distinct from incident response itself, with real-incident experience treated as supplemental rather than a substitute; the outcome depends on the assessor.
- **Where a stricter CUI enclave sits inside a broader facility that handles only federal contract information, and CUI is spilled into the broader environment by an external party, response typically treats the spill as the sender's responsibility to remediate, uses detection controls to catch spills into the lower environment, and documents a response procedure (containment, notification where possible, migration of the material) in the lower environment's own policies rather than in the CUI enclave's system security plan.** A distinct incident response capability is still maintained and tested for the CUI enclave itself.

## Evidence examples

- **Documented scenario and facilitator notes from a tabletop exercise or walkthrough.** [a]
- **Filled-out incident response forms completed during the exercise.** [a]
- **Lessons-learned writeup and any change requests opened as a result.** [a]
- **Test schedule or calendar entry showing exercise cadence.** [a]
- **Externally published tabletop exercise scenario packages used as a starting point for the internal exercise.** [a]

## Related requirements

- **3.6.1** supplies the capability this requirement tests; a test cannot exercise a capability that was never established.
- **3.6.2** tracking and reporting steps are typically part of what a realistic exercise walks through, since the scenario should include notification, not only technical containment.
- **3.3.1** log content and retention, since a realistic test checks whether the logging in place would actually surface the scenario's activity.
