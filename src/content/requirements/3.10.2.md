## Requirement

"Protect and monitor the physical facility and support infrastructure for organizational systems."

## Objectives

[a] the physical facility where organizational systems reside is protected;
[b] the support infrastructure for organizational systems is protected;
[c] the physical facility where organizational systems reside is monitored; and
[d] the support infrastructure for organizational systems is monitored.

## Sufficient

- **Badge or card-controlled access to a server room or data center, limited to a defined access list.** [a][b]
- **A badge-controlled wiring closet with alerting when an unauthorized badge is presented.** An alert generated by the access system is itself treated as satisfying the monitoring half of the objective, not just the protection half. [a][c][d]
- **For a small office, door locks and a limited access list covering most of the day**, with a staffed reception area during business hours and a perimeter alarm covering the rest. [a][c]
- **A key sign-in and sign-out log for a locked wiring or electrical room**, or an electronic lock paired with a central monitoring notification, satisfying protection and monitoring together. [b][d]
- **CUI stored in a locked cabinet inside a locked, unmanned office**, without a camera pointed at the cabinet itself. Routine building access, such as janitorial staff, is acceptable as long as the cabinet stays locked and any alarm gets armed on exit. [a][c]
- **A home office with a manual lock on the door, network equipment secured in a locked cabinet within that room, and encrypted end devices.** Accepted as a remote employee's facility-level implementation. [a][b]
- **General facility security, such as a locked, access-controlled building, covering wiring that runs through shared space like a warehouse.** [b]

## Insufficient

- **In-scope equipment sitting in a shared server room with no access control and no way to determine who has been in it.** This trends toward not met rather than met. [a][c]
- **A building supervisor or other building tenants holding unrestricted access to network closets.** The facility's protection has to actually exclude people outside the organization's own control, not just look controlled on paper. [a]
- **Substituting a logical, network-layer control, such as port security, for physical protection.** The objective is about the physical facility and its infrastructure, not what happens at the network layer. [a][b]
- **Assuming a Level 1 network handling FCI no longer needs its own physical security once a separate Level 2 enclave exists elsewhere.** The Level 1 obligation for that network does not go away. [a]

## Over-engineering

- **Securing the power utility itself, or vetting the personnel who guard a power plant.** "Support infrastructure" is read as the facility's own internal infrastructure, such as wiring and distribution, not the wider utility grid feeding it. [b]
- **Building in redundant power supplies, fail-closed door systems, or guaranteed uptime.** NIST SP 800-171 does not carry forward the availability-focused physical controls, such as power, humidity, and fire suppression, present in the broader control catalog it derives from. [a][b]
- **Treating continuous, 24/7 staffed camera and alarm monitoring as a hard baseline.** Monitoring can be satisfied through a mix of access-system alerting, periodic camera review, and after-hours alarms rather than around-the-clock live monitoring. [c][d]
- **Pointing a camera at CUI already locked in a cabinet inside a locked, unmanned room.** That level of control matches classified-information handling, not the moderate protection CUI calls for. [c]

## Edge cases

- **A landlord-controlled demarcation point the organization cannot fully restrict.** Assessors have still wanted visibility into who can reach it even though full control is not feasible; whether that extends to requiring a third-party physical security provider's own certification is unsettled, and the outcome depends on the assessor.
- **Whether a power loss would cause a security control to fail is the operative question, not whether the power source itself is protected.** If doors fail open when power is lost and locked doors were what kept CUI's confidentiality intact, that is a real gap; if network equipment loses power but CUI stays behind encrypted devices and locks that do not depend on that power, confidentiality holds regardless.
- **A dedicated, fully locked room built specifically for CUI processing**, closer to a classified-handling model, is an accepted, heavier way to meet this requirement for organizations that want that level of assurance, though it is not what the objective requires as a floor.

## Evidence examples

- **Badge access logs for server rooms and restricted equipment areas.** [a][c]
- **Door access logs with a documented periodic review.** [a][c]
- **Key sign-in and sign-out logs for locked wiring or electrical rooms.** [b][d]
- **Perimeter alarm system records covering after-hours entry and exit.** [c][d]
- **Documentation or photos showing home-office network equipment secured in a locked cabinet inside a locked room.** [a][b]

## Related requirements

- **3.10.1** establishes who is authorized to be in the facility; this requirement protects and monitors the facility and its infrastructure itself.
- **3.10.5** covers the keys, badges, and access devices this requirement's locks and card readers depend on.
- **3.13.8** already requires encrypting CUI in transit or physically enclosing the transmission line, so this requirement's infrastructure protection does not need to separately re-secure cabling encryption.
- **3.10.6** carries the same protect-and-monitor intent to alternate work sites this requirement's facility-based controls do not reach.
