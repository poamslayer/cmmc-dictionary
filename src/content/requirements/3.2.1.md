## Requirement

"Ensure that managers, systems administrators, and users of organizational systems are made aware of the security risks associated with their activities and of the applicable policies, standards, and procedures related to the security of those systems."

## Objectives

[a] security risks associated with organizational activities involving CUI are identified;
[b] policies, standards, and procedures related to the security of the system are identified;
[c] managers, systems administrators, and users of the system are made aware of the security risks associated with their activities; and
[d] managers, systems administrators, and users of the system are made aware of the applicable policies, standards, and procedures related to the security of the system.

## Sufficient

- **A government-sourced awareness module used as one piece of a larger program, layered with material written about the organization's own environment.** Assessors have accepted the government module as a component but not as the whole program, since it does not by itself describe the organization's policies or procedures. [c][d]
- **A short, organization-specific briefing, as small as a few slides, covering where the policies and procedures live, who the security point of contact is, and a place to acknowledge the material.** This minimum format has been accepted as sufficient. [b][d]
- **An employee handbook that folds in the acceptable-use terms and is re-signed on a recurring basis.** Accepted when the acceptable-use content sits inside the signed document rather than existing only as a separate, unsigned reference. [c][d]
- **A standalone acceptable-use agreement, signed apart from the handbook.** Equally accepted as a delivery method. [c][d]
- **A directory platform's built-in terms-of-use capability, presenting the acceptable-use material at sign-in and requiring renewed consent on a recurring basis, with consent logged centrally.** This pairs delivery with a record of who accepted it. [c][d]
- **Email delivery paired with a poll or acknowledgment step that the recipient has to complete**, rather than a message sent with no response expected. [c]
- **Verification through interview.** When staff can describe, in conversation, the applicable policies and procedures and where to find them, that performance has supported a MET finding on its own. [c][d]
- **Retained completion records**, such as stored certificates or reports pulled from a training system, kept where they can be produced later. [c]

## Insufficient

- **A government-sourced module used alone, with nothing added about the organization's own environment.** It does not name the organization's policies, standards, or procedures, so it cannot reach [b] or [d] by itself.
- **Generic cyber-awareness content with no organization-specific layer added.** The same gap applies: awareness of a general risk category is not awareness of this organization's rules.
- **A plain email announcement with no way to confirm anyone engaged with it.** A message nobody has to respond to is too easy to overlook, and produces nothing to show a reviewer afterward. [c]
- **A sign-off from the IT function asserting that everyone completed training, without the standing to compel every employee to take it.** IT rarely has the organizational authority to direct company-wide compliance, and a reviewer may look past the signature to test whether it reflects something real. [c][d]
- **Training content that stops at general government material and never states what the organization itself expects of its people.** This fails [d], which is specifically about the organization's own policies, standards, and procedures.

## Over-engineering

No documented position beyond the Assessment Guide; see confidence.

## Edge cases

- **Users who only touch government-furnished equipment.** Where that equipment is owned and operated by the government and carried in the government's own system security plan, the training obligation for it sits with the government, not the contractor. [c][d]
- **Users who hold both a government-furnished account and a contractor account.** If the contractor account is logically separated from the CUI environment through permissions, the person may fall into a lower-scoping category rather than needing full training coverage; having them sign the acceptable-use material anyway is a low-cost way to head off a stricter reviewer. [a][c]
- **Whether training has to repeat every year.** The wording "are made aware" does not itself set a cadence, so a strict reading could be met with a single pass. In practice, reviewers lean toward an annual repeat and treat a one-time pass as thin due diligence, even though it is arguably defensible; the outcome depends on the assessor.
- **Whether "standards" needs its own separate document.** The requirement asks for awareness of policies, standards, and procedures together; standard-type content can live inside the SSP or a procedure document rather than as its own artifact, as long as the organization can point to what its governing documents are. [b]
- **Who counts as a "user of the system."** The requirement reaches everyone who touches the system, not only the people who handle CUI directly, and includes managers and administrators alongside end users. [c][d]

## Evidence examples

- **Stored completion certificates**, in PDF or similar form, kept in a repository. [c]
- **Reports pulled from a training or learning-management system**, showing completion by person. [c]
- **A signed employee handbook** containing the acceptable-use and security policy content. [c][d]
- **A standalone signed acceptable-use policy**, apart from the handbook. [c][d]
- **Directory terms-of-use consent records**, showing acknowledgment at login and on renewal. [c][d]
- **Email acknowledgment or poll responses** tied to a training send. [c]
- **A separate privileged-user agreement**, signed by administrative account holders apart from the general population. [c][d]
- **A short organization-specific briefing deck** with a recorded acknowledgment step. [b][c][d]

## Related requirements

- **3.2.2** covers the deeper, role-specific training for people who carry information-security duties; this requirement is the broader, organization-wide awareness layer everyone gets.
- **3.2.3** narrows the same awareness obligation to one specific risk area, insider-threat indicators, and can often be delivered through the same program.
- **3.1.1** and **3.1.2** define who is authorized and what they may do; this requirement is how those people learn what the rules actually are.
- **3.12.4**, the SSP, is the natural home for identifying what an organization's policies, standards, and procedures are, which this requirement then requires making people aware of.
